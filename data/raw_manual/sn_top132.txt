What is REDCap?
Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) is an online, customisable data capture tool. It provides the ideal means to gather data in a secure environment and avoids the need to enter data into spreadsheets or use paper-based forms and surveys. At its most basic, you can use the REDCap functions to build data collection forms consisting of "variables" which take the appearance of text boxes, check boxes, dropdown menus, and so on. These forms can also be made accessible over the internet to research participants as "surveys". Filling out these forms enters data directly into a secure database on the REDCap server. Reasons to use REDCap Fast and flexible – building a project from scratch is a simple matter of point-and-click using your internet browser. Clean data – REDCap makes it easy to collect clean data as your data collection forms can be standardised and include validation and data quality rules. Suitable for "highly protected" data – data is stored securely, is encrypted in transit and at rest, and resides on University-managed servers within NSW. Unless you export data to your personal device, the data is always stored securely on these servers. REDCap has been approved by ICT as suitable for data classified as "highly protected" under the University's data classifications. Secure collaboration – both UniKey holders and external collaborators can benefit from simultaneously accessing data on REDCap in a secure, encrypted manner. Access to specific data and functions in REDCap are highly customisable for each user. Import and export – even if your data isn't currently in REDCap, you can easily build a suitable project and bulk upload existing data to ensure it meets relevant data handling requirements. Data can be exported to CSV, PDF, SAS, Stata, R, or SPSS for analysis. Identifying information can be removed with one click if required. Powerful – REDCap can be used to manage longitudinal clinical trials and includes features such as audit trails, randomisation, scheduling, data access groups, and a data resolution workflow module, amongst others. Free and supported – The University's installation of REDCap is maintained by ICT specifically for University of Sydney researchers and their affiliates. Functional support and training are available from Sydney Informatics Hub. Both services are free-of-charge. There is also a highly-engaged community of REDCap users both internationally and within the University (via our Yammer group). How can I enter data in REDCap when I don't have an internet connection? Do I need to back up my REDCap project? Am I able to create a multi-language project (containing forms in different languages)? Who can access REDCap? REDCap is available to all University staff, students and affiliates who hold a UniKey, for non-commercial purposes. External users (both institutional and non-institutional, including NSW Health) can gain access to the University's REDCap if they are working on a project with a University of Sydney researcher. External users cannot use the University's REDCap for their own purposes. External users may join projects created by UniKey holders but may not create new projects themselves, as our licence agreement with the vendor of REDCap stipulates that only UniKey holders can create new projects. What is the Research Data Store (RDS)?
The Research Data Store (RDS) is an enterprise grade Network Attached Storage (NAS) device, which has a large and expandable storage capacity. Once granted access, users can simply use their Unikey credentials to map a network drive to their allocated storage space on the RDS NAS. This will allow users to access their storage space from any computer on the university network or off-campus via VPN. Researchers are required to have a Research Data Management Plan and have agreed to the Research Data Management Policy Declaration to be able to use the Research Data Store. This is performed by creating a new project within the Researcher Dashboard (DashR). Creating a DashR project helps the University to better understand its research data, maintain the RDS service into the future and align itself to broader national programs to make research data discoverable and re-usable. Numerous measures are in place to ensure your data is safe on the RDS. Redundant hardware: The RDS itself is hosted on an enterprise-grade storage platform, which has inbuilt redundancy at different hardware levels. Backup: RDS data is backed up on a regular basis. Nightly backups are kept for seven days, weekly backups are kept for four weeks and monthly backups are kept for three months. Disaster Recovery (DR): The RDS service will be accessible to researchers in a disaster scenario. All projects are guaranteed up to 2TB data storage without requiring additional approval; only projects that exceed 2TB will need to be reviewed. If your project needs more storage than your current quota, please contact your respective Faculty Research Manager (FRM).

What is the University code repository?
The University Code Repository is a software repository management platform using an enterprise edition of GitHub. It was implemented as a university-wide resource to solve problems collaboratively and isn't just for coding: it is a platform for any complex, creative work requiring strong version control and collaborative input at the University of Sydney. Collaboration With GitHub Enterprise, code collaboration and review are built into the development process. Teams can share work, discuss changes, and get feedback all in one place. Pull requests let users propose changes, share ideas about code, and learn from each other—all in a clean, timeline-style interface. With everyone working together on one platform, users can gain greater visibility in to their projects. An ElasticSearch cluster allows any user to find code that already exists across the University. Leveraging existing code saves time, and if it is improved along the way, everyone wins. Monitoring and Dashboards: Allows users to gain visibility into work across their projects and teams. GitHub Enterprise is free for staff, students and affiliates. Getting started: Visit https://github.sydney.edu.au and enter your UniKey and UniKey password to login. The platform can be accessed from anywhere in the world. The code repository uses the open source Git-distributed version control system with the GitHub management application, and it's not just for storing code. Documents, blogs, research papers, and other text-based work can use the platform for version control, sharing, and collaboration. The Code Repository is protected by the University's internal firewalls and additionally, SSH is enabled. The platform operates in high availability mode which is designed to minimise any service disruption. Two-factor authentication (2FA) is supported.

How do I use file encryption?
All University-workstations or devices storing Highly Protected data are required to be encrypted as per the Cyber Security Standard – Data Handling and Cyber Security Standard – Security Hardening. For devices under University management, encryption should be delivered as part of the Secure Baseline for all endpoints. In some cases, users may need to manually encrypt if they are using a non-University managed device and are handling Highly Protected data. For example, users that are part of research teams that are using personal devices to analyse, access or otherwise store Highly Protected data. File encryption is a method of securing a file or series of files from unauthorised access and tampering. Typically, these solutions work by turning a folder into a container of files that, without a special application and password, will be inaccessible. Depending on the software used, the container can be called a vault, volume or archive. Cryptomator is a free file encryption solution that works by encrypting a folder you specify as a "vault." It encrypts folders and files directly as well as encrypting file or folder names, due to this, it is significantly easier to sync with cloud storage as it is only going to sync file by file, as opposed to synchronising a complete container or archive. VeraCrypt is free encryption software which you can download from their website. VeraCrypt is specifically useful for when you need to encrypt cloud storage or a shared drive as a requirement of an ethics approval or similar and you are unable to store it locally as well. A sufficiently strong password has a minimum length of 13 alphabetic characters, or a minimum length of 10 characters consisting of at least three of the following character sets.

How do I recognise phishing emails and what should I do?
Phishing is a psychological attack used by malicious individuals to trick you into giving up information or taking an action. Phishing originally described attacks simply 'fishing' for your username and password. The term has now evolved to cover almost any message-based attack (including via social media and instant messaging). These attacks begin with an attacker sending a message pretending to be from someone or something you know or recognise. These messages then entice you into taking an action such as clicking on a malicious link, opening a malicious attachment or responding to a scam. How do I spot phishing? Names that don't match the email address (e.g. 'University of Sydney HR' sending from a gmail account). Emails with a strange reply address that doesn't match the From: address. Alarming tones, threats of account closure and immediate action. Bad grammar and misspellings. Generic placeholder style language such as 'Dear Customer'. Websites with an unusual web address. Spear phishing: The concept of 'spear phishing' is the same as 'phishing', except that instead of sending as many emails as possible for maximum gain, the attack will send a specially crafted email to very select individuals. What should I do if I receive a phishing email? Do not respond and do not click any links! Immediately report the email by using the 'Report Message' function in Outlook, or report to the email address phishing@sydney.edu.au. If you believe you have fallen victim to an attack, immediately change your UniKey password and contact the University Shared Service Centre on +61 2 9351 2000.

How do I use OneDrive?
OneDrive is a cloud file storage service that lets you share, synchronise and collaborate on documents, from any device that supports the app. As part of the University Office 365 implementation, staff have free access to a OneDrive account with 3000GB storage to store your work documents. OneDrive features: Access anywhere: once stored on your University OneDrive account, your documents can be opened from your phone, tablet, laptop or browser. You can access your OneDrive outside campus without a VPN connection. Data security: mechanisms are in place to ensure only you and your collaborators can see your files. Save space: choose the files and folders you want to synchronise to your device, or use the Files On-Demand feature to intelligently download files as you use them. Version history and recovery: deleted OneDrive documents are kept for seven years, allowing you to easily recover files and restore previous version. All staff can access their OneDrive as part of their Office 365 account. Office 365 is available to all staff with their University email address. You can access your University OneDrive account online, on your phones, tablets and laptops. Online: log into the Office 365 portal with your University email address, then open the OneDrive app. Devices: download OneDrive for your laptops, Android or iOS devices. ICT's Information Security team has conducted an extensive security assessment of Office 365, and has given approval for it to be used for highly confidential data in many cases, provided it has been encrypted. OneDrive data is stored on Australian servers. The only people who can access your files are you, the collaborators whom with you selectively share your files, and system administrators. You can access your University OneDrive account from your browser or your devices, from any location on and off campus. All you need is an active internet connection to access University OneDrive when you are off campus - no VPN is required.

What is Highly Protected Data?
During the University's day-to-day operations, we may collect or process sensitive information that could have serious implications for individuals, third parties, or the University. Based in the degree of sensitivity of the information and the risk associated with any loss of confidentiality, all University data is classified into either Public, Protected, or Highly Protected security classifications in line with our Cyber Security Standard - Data Security. The classification of data determines how that data should be managed - how it should be transferred, stored and accessed. Highly protected data: Data that is created, collected, stored, processed, sent or received by the University is classified as "Highly Protected" if loss of confidentiality of the research data would result in serious economic/financial, or reputation impact to an individual or the University, or result in a notifiable privacy breach. Examples of highly protected data include: Health information that allows identification of individuals; Identifiable information relating to an individual's ethnic or racial origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs; Information about the nature and location of a place or an item of Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander cultural significance; Confidential commercial agreements; Ecological information that may place vulnerable species or ecological communities at risk. Protected data: the information is not generally available to the public, or loss of confidentiality of the research data would have a material impact on the University or an individual. Examples include unpublished research data and results, personal information other than sensitive personal information. Public data: it is generally available to the public, it does not have a material impact on the University or an individual in the event of loss of information.

How do I secure my Windows device?
Staff are required to encrypt their devices to ensure their data is protected. While users should be saving their data on the University's cloud storage solutions, your computer will need to be encrypted to secure synced files from these services. Securing a Dell University-owned device: Before you start, make sure you have undertaken the following: The machine must be on the University's MCS domain. Save and close any documents you are working on. Back up all your data. Do this on campus. You will need to have Administrator access to your device to undertake this process. Dual boot laptops are not supported and should not be used to store protected data. Select the Start Menu. Search for BitLocker. Select Manage BitLocker. Click Turn on BitLocker. The BitLocker Wizard will determine if your computer is able to run BitLocker and will run through the required steps to enable encryption. Securing a personal Windows device: Personal devices can also be encrypted although the encryption key will not be saved on the University's Active Directory. If you experience any issues please contact the Shared Service Centre.

How do we block spam and malicious emails at the University?
We use both Mimecast and Microsoft filtering as a frontline defence to stop spam and malicious emails being delivered to your mailbox. This is done in the following ways: Quarantining emails suspected to be spam. Attachments may be removed from emails if deemed malicious. URLs for external sites sent within emails are rewritten. Recipients reporting specific emails that appear to be spam, phishing and indicating what is not-spam. You can access quarantined email by opening the spam notification email which allows you to release, block or permit emails. You will receive a notification once a day between 12pm and 1pm if you have captured items. From the notification you have the option to release mail that has been mis-identified as spam. Spam is automatically deleted after 14 days, but if you would like to block spam that has been quarantined you can do this from the email. Release – This will release the current email on Hold to your Inbox. Permit – This delivers the email to your Inbox and adds the sender's address to your personal Permit list. Block – This rejects the email and adds the sender's address to your personal Block list to block future emails from this sender. Mimecast transforms URLs sent in email as a way of scanning external websites. This only applies to URLs for external sites and non-University services. Hover your mouse over an external site URL in an email and you will see the URL has been rewritten with the prefix 'protect-au.mimecast.com'. This indicates the URL will be scanned first by Mimecast before directing you to the site. Microsoft Outlook allows you to report spam, phishing and not-spam emails via the menu option 'Report Message'.

What is a Virtual Machine (VM)?
A Virtual Machine (VM) is a flexible, hosted computing environment. It is an emulation of a computer system containing an operating system, memory, processing power and all the functionality you can find in a physical desktop or server. You can access a VM's capabilities via any computer, without needing physical storage space for this VM. You can add software and change operating systems in a VM (just like your physical desktop). VMs are available for staff. If you select a standard VM, you can expect to receive your VM in as little as three working days. What are the differences between High-Performance Computing and Virtual Machines? A VM is: A hosted compute environment with a generic operating system, where the researcher can install any tools at their discretion. The specifications are low to mid-range performance, and ICT supports the operating system and networking aspects. The end user is responsible for maintaining applications and data flows. VMs are great for hosting long term and persistent applications, developing software, and building data workflows. HPC is: High-throughput computers for solving computational problems and processing data. It can run jobs with hundreds of cores or large memory requirements, and is configured for fast access to storage. There are massive amounts of resources available on the system and resources are provided through a batch queue. The VMs for Researchers service is designed to provide persistent hosting for more than large scale computation. The service provides: A hosted environment for your research; Accessible to your collaborators - via UniKey; Integration with the Research Data Store (RDS); Capability to install your software; Backup, maintenance and security options.

What is CloudStor and how do I use it?
CloudStor is an online data storage service (similar to Dropbox or Google Drive) that makes sharing your data easy. Built with high performance computers for Australian researchers, it is designed to accommodate your large data storage needs. As a researcher you are eligible for 100 GB of free storage with additional storage available via paid subscription. This service is best for active research data that may need to be shared between multiple people. For other long term data storage needs, please read the article What is the Research Data Store (RDS)? or contact your IT Manager. How can I access CloudStor? Go to the CloudStor website - https://cloudstor.aarnet.edu.au. Select The University of Sydney from the list of institutions. The Australian Access Federation - AAF Login displays. Log in with your UniKey and UniKey password. Click Continue. You can then upload documents to CloudStor and when you have finished, log out. CloudStor offers a data synchronising client that can be installed on your computer (Windows, Mac and Linux). The software is called ownCloud. This sync client allows you to set up a folder on your computer to synchronise data to a selected folder on CloudStor.
